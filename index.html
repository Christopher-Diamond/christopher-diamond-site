I am Senior Lecturer in Hindi-Urdu and South Asian Studies at the Australian National University, where I convene the Hindi-Urdu, South Asian Studies and Tibetan programs. My research examines the literature, music and manuscript cultures of premodern North and East India.

Much of this work concerns Mithila, a region in northern Bihar and southern Nepal, and its language, Maithili. At its centre are the lyric poems (padas) of Vidyāpati (c. 1370–1450), a poet of the Mithila court whose songs were later adopted by the Malla kings of Nepal and by Vaiṣṇava devotional communities in Bengal. A new edition and translation of the oldest manuscripts of these songs is currently in preparation, together with a study of how kings and brahmins in Nepal and Bihar used them to project power and prestige.

After training in Hindi and music at SOAS, University of London, I completed my MA and PhD in South Asian Languages and Literature at the University of Washington. Fieldwork and archival research have taken me to Bihar, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and Nepal for more than a decade. My sources are in Maithili, Hindi (including Braj Bhāṣā and Avadhī), Bengali, Urdu, Sanskrit, Persian and Apabhraṃśa, and my other research interests include bhakti literature, performance, and eastern Indian versions of the epics and Purāṇas.

Alongside teaching and research, I co-founded ANUBhasha, a collective for the digitisation and digital repatriation of premodern South Asian texts, and co-chair the Mithila Studies Network. Public writing on South Asian languages and literature is another part of this work.

selected publications

  1. AE
    The ‘Indescribable Country’: Locating Mithila between Bengal and the Hindi Heartland in Raghunandana Das’s Mithilānāṭaka
    Christopher L. Diamond
    Asian Ethnology, 2026
    Special issue: Critical Contours of Mithila Studies. Open access
    Abs DOI Bib HTML PDF

    This article examines the way multilingualism was employed by Raghunandana Das in his 1923 play, the “Drama of Mithila” (mithilānāṭaka), to shift Mithila (the region) and Maithili (the language) from its historically marginal position in the social geography of the newly emerging Indian nation. Since the late nineteenth century up to the present, Maithil authors and elites engaged in promoting their language and cultural region on a national stage found themselves disadvantaged and caught between the hegemony of the Hindi heartland and the political and cultural powerhouse of Bengal. In this context, Raghunandana Das’s moralistic drama written in Maithili, Hindi, Bengali, and Sanskrit renegotiates Maithili/Mithila’s marginal status by emphasizing its engagement with multiple elite cultural milieux—the national, the Sanskritic/traditional, and the modern. For Das, this renegotiation works to leverage the region’s credentials as an independent cultural sphere from which others might take influence.

  2. AE
    Guest Editors’ Introduction: Defining and Contextualizing Mithila Studies
    Pranav Prakash, Christopher L. Diamond, and Coralynn V. Davis
    Asian Ethnology, 2026
    Open access
    Abs DOI Bib HTML PDF

    Our introduction establishes “Mithila Studies” as a new field of academic inquiry focused on the cultures, communities, and histories of Maithili-speaking peoples worldwide. We critique the limitations of previous “Maithili Studies” approaches, which have privileged upper-caste Hindu male perspectives, and which are mired in the problems of colonialist historiography, Orientalist philology and postcolonial communalism. We envision an inclusive, ethical, and critical field of inquiry that empowers marginalized voices and experiences, especially those of women, lower castes, and religious minorities; challenges colonial and nationalist construction of regional identities; explores Mithila’s liminal status in global cultures and academies; and posits Mithila subjects as viable bases of multifaceted research projects. By engaging diverse disciplinary approaches, archives, and communities, we seek to develop Mithila Studies as a field that resists parochialism, promotes comparative analysis and collaborative research, and provides new perspectives on key issues in the humanities and social sciences.

  3. MSS
    The Strange Afterlife of Vidyāpati Ṭhākura (ca. 1350–1450 CE): Anthological Manuscripts, Linguistic Confusion, and Religious Appropriation
    Christopher L. Diamond
    Manuscript Studies, 2019
    Open access
    Abs Bib HTML PDF

    This article examines the difficult aspects of working with anthological manuscripts and printed editions of lyrical vernacular poetry in South Asia by focusing on the textual reception of Vidyāpati Ṭhākura. In his own life, Vidyāpati wrote technical treatises in Sanskrit, historical narratives in Apabhraṃśa, and a corpus of lyrical poems (padas) and two dramas in the vernacular Maithili language. While his technical works remained relatively static and limited in their circulation, Vidyāpati’s lyrical poems had a more enduring and geographically widespread effect on the languages, literatures, and religions of Mithilā and Eastern India (Bengal, Orissa, and Assam). The anthologies of padas, usually called "padāvalī-s", whatever their historical manifestation or locality, were usually collections of disconnected padas without contextual narratives or explanations. This analysis focuses on the difficulty of working with free-standing small lyrical poems, which were never conceived of as unified textual entities, in both organized padāvalīs and small disposable manuscript handbooks (pothī-s). The padas were used pragmatically by elite poets, devotional saints, and musicians from the 15th century CE onwards. This creates problems when one tries to trace physical remains and textual sources from this period. There exists a gap between the Maithili padāvalīs of the 16th and 17th centuries and the Bengali Vaiṣṇava padāvalīs writing in a hybrid Bengali-Maithili kuntsprache of the 18th and 19th centuries. Since the linguistic and poetic variations and total number of attestations are so extensive, what relationship can be inferred between the Maithili padāvalī tradition and later anthologies based on manuscript and other textual evidence? I argue for a strategy of closely reading the variances and additions to the bhaṇitā (poetic signature) that reveal an appeal to the courtly prestige of Mithilā, even in the devotional communities of Bengal.
